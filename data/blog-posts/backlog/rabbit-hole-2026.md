# The Rabbit Hole Starts With One Question

> *The funny thing about curiosity is that it rarely stops where you expect it to.*

One of the questions I get asked surprisingly often is why I switched to Linux, why I use GrapheneOS, or why I've been gradually replacing large parts of my software stack with open-source alternatives.

The honest answer is that I never planned to.

I didn't wake up one morning and decide to de-Google my life. I wasn't trying to become a privacy advocate, nor was I looking for an excuse to replace every piece of proprietary software I used. In fact, I was perfectly happy using mainstream tools as long as they solved the problem I needed them to solve.

What changed wasn't my philosophy.

It was my curiosity.

---

## It Started With Windows

Like most people, I had been using Windows for years without thinking too much about it. It was simply the operating system that happened to be installed on my computer.

Then I started noticing little things.

Why did shutting the machine down sometimes take so long? Why did startup occasionally involve network activity before I had even opened a browser? Why did every major update seem to introduce another background service or quietly reset settings that I had previously changed?

The repeated reinstallation of Microsoft Copilot after updates became almost symbolic. I would remove it, another update would arrive, and there it was again. Over time it stopped feeling like *my* operating system and started feeling like an operating system that I merely happened to use.

None of these observations proved anything on their own. They simply made me realize how little I actually understood about what my computer was doing.

That realization bothered me.

---

## Looking Beyond the Desktop

Instead of asking whether Windows was "good" or "bad", I started asking a much simpler question.

**What is actually happening while my computer is running?**

What services are active?

What leaves my machine over the network?

What is collected?

What is synchronized?

What happens during startup?

What happens during shutdown?

These questions eventually led me to monitoring network traffic, reviewing permissions, reading documentation, and paying closer attention to the software I relied on every day. I wasn't searching for conspiracy theories—I was simply trying to understand the technology that I spent most of my day using.

The more I learned, the more I realized that visibility matters.

---

## Linux Was a Consequence, Not the Goal

Moving to Arch Linux wasn't a statement against Windows.

It was the natural consequence of asking questions that I wanted answers to.

Linux gave me something I hadn't realized I was missing: transparency.

I could inspect services, understand what started during boot, review configuration files, monitor network traffic, and decide which components I actually wanted running on my machine. More importantly, it gave me confidence that I could understand my own system instead of simply trusting that everything was behaving as expected.

That doesn't make Linux objectively better for everyone.

It simply aligned better with what I was looking for.

---

## Then My Phone Started Asking the Same Questions

Once I had started paying attention to my computer, it became difficult not to notice similar patterns elsewhere.

My phone spends far more time with me than my desktop ever does, yet I understood even less about what it was doing.

Why was there network activity while the phone was sitting idle?

Why did applications continue appearing in background process lists after I had closed them?

Why did disabling permissions not always seem to stop background behaviour?

Again, I wasn't assuming malicious intent.

I simply wanted to understand what was happening.

That curiosity eventually led me to GrapheneOS.

Like Linux, it wasn't a decision motivated by ideology. It was motivated by the desire for greater transparency, stronger security, and a system that gives users more control over their own devices.

---

## One Question Led to Another

Looking back, every change I made followed the same pattern.

One question inevitably led to another.

If I questioned my operating system, why not my phone?

If I questioned my phone, why not the applications running on it?

If I questioned those applications, why not the cloud services they depended on?

Eventually I found myself replacing software one application at a time—not because I wanted to build the ultimate privacy setup, but because each replacement solved a problem I had already identified.

Signal replaced messaging platforms that collected more data than I was comfortable sharing.

Bitwarden replaced browser-based password storage.

GrapheneOS replaced Android.

Arch Linux replaced Windows.

Eventually I started self-hosting services where it made sense.

None of these changes happened overnight.

Each was simply the logical conclusion of the previous question.

---

## Curiosity, Not Paranoia

One thing I've become increasingly careful about is avoiding the trap of treating privacy as an all-or-nothing problem.

I still use cloud services.

I still use proprietary software.

I still choose convenience when the trade-off makes sense.

This isn't about rejecting technology.

It's about understanding it.

For me, digital sovereignty isn't the absence of technology—it's the ability to make informed decisions about the technology I choose to depend on.

Curiosity was simply the first step.
